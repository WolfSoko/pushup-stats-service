<h2>The short answer — and why it's not enough</h2>
<p>
  Ask ten people at the gym which muscles a push-up trains, and you'll get the same answer ten times:
  chest, triceps, shoulders. That's true. But it's about as complete as saying a car consists of an
  engine, wheels, and a steering wheel. A clean push-up is a full-body exercise in which roughly a dozen
  muscle groups, from the finger flexors down to the toes, are involved with clearly divided roles — and
  depending on where you place your hands, how high your feet are, and how deep you go, the workload
  shifts noticeably between them.
</p>
<p>
  This article gets to the bottom of the question: who's actually doing the pushing? Who's
  stabilizing? How much weight are you really moving? And what do the electromyography (EMG) studies of
  the last twenty years say about tailoring the exercise to specific muscles? If you're just looking for
  the execution, you'll find it in the
  <a href="/en/wiki/liegestuetz-typen/standard">wiki entry for the standard push-up</a>. Here, it's
  about the why behind it.
</p>

<h2>The three main players: who does the work</h2>
<p>
  Mechanically, a push-up is a horizontal press — the same movement as a bench press, except you move
  your own body instead of a barbell. Three muscles supply the bulk of the force.
</p>

<h3>Pectoralis major (chest)</h3>
<p>
  The pectoralis major is the fan-shaped muscle that runs from the collarbone and sternum to the upper
  arm. Its main job in the push-up is <strong>horizontal adduction</strong> — it pulls the upper arm
  across the front of the body as you push away from the floor. It has two parts: the
  <em>clavicular</em> (upper) portion, which works harder with elevated feet and a slightly upward
  pressing angle, and the larger <em>sternal</em> (middle/lower) portion, which dominates in the
  classic and incline push-up. In the bottom position, the chest is most stretched and simultaneously
  under the highest load — the moment the strongest growth stimulus occurs.
</p>

<h3>Triceps (triceps brachii)</h3>
<p>
  The three-headed arm extensor is responsible for <strong>elbow extension</strong>. It handles mostly
  the second half of the upward movement, once the chest has already left its mechanically favorable
  range. The narrower your hands, the greater its share. The triceps makes up roughly two-thirds of
  upper-arm volume — anyone who wants "big arms" can't skip it, and narrow push-ups are one of the best
  equipment-free exercises for it.
</p>

<h3>Anterior deltoid (front shoulder)</h3>
<p>
  The front portion of the shoulder musculature assists the chest in moving the arm forward and
  stabilizes the shoulder joint throughout the rep. With elevated feet (decline) and during pike
  push-ups, the load shifts noticeably here — the pike push-up is essentially a vertical pressing
  movement, making it the bodyweight version of the shoulder press.
</p>

<h2>The silent helpers: who stabilizes</h2>
<p>
  What distinguishes the push-up from the bench press isn't the pressing motion — it's everything
  around it. On a bench, your core lies supported; here, you have to hold it yourself. That's exactly
  why the exercise activates muscles that stay dormant during bench press.
</p>
<ul>
  <li>
    <strong>Serratus anterior ("boxer's muscle").</strong> It sits along the side of the ribcage under
    the shoulder blade and pulls it forward (protraction). In the top position, as you actively push the
    floor away and the upper back rounds slightly, it works hardest. The "push-up plus" variant — a
    deliberately exaggerated protraction at the end of every rep — has been used in shoulder
    rehabilitation since a
    <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Ludewig+2004+push-up+plus+serratus+anterior" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow ugc">study by Ludewig et al. (2004)</a>,
    because it engages the serratus more strongly than almost any other exercise. A strong serratus is
    the best insurance against shoulder problems during pressing.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Core (rectus abdominis, obliques, spinal erectors).</strong> Your midsection holds you in a
    straight line — the push-up is essentially a plank with moving arms.
    <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Freeman+2006+quantifying+muscle+patterns+spine+load+push-up" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow ugc">Freeman et al. (2006)</a>
    showed that core activation during a push-up is comparable to that of a forearm plank, and clearly
    higher for unstable or one-arm variants.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Glutes and quads.</strong> The gluteus maximus and quadriceps keep the hip and knee
    extended. They aren't trained for growth in the process, but without their engagement the hip sags —
    the most common form breakdown of all (more in
    <a href="/en/blog/pushup-mistakes">the 7 most common push-up mistakes</a>).
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Rotator cuff.</strong> Four small muscles that keep the head of the upper arm centered in the
    socket. They work isometrically on every rep — and they're the reason elbow angle matters so much
    (more on that shortly).
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Forearms and hands.</strong> The finger flexors and wrist stabilizers distribute load across
    the palm. Anyone who trains push-ups at higher volume develops noticeably stronger forearms — and
    anyone who neglects them gets wrist pain (see
    <a href="/en/blog/pushup-wrist-shoulder-pain">Pain During Push-Ups</a>).
  </li>
</ul>

<h2>How much weight are you really moving?</h2>
<p>
  "Just bodyweight" sounds harmless. Measurements on force plates say otherwise. Two research groups —
  <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Ebben+2011+kinetic+analysis+push-up+variations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow ugc">Ebben et al. (2011)</a>
  and
  <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Suprak+2011+percentage+body+mass+supported+push-up" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow ugc">Suprak et al. (2011)</a>

<p>
  Two things stand out. First: a standard push-up for an 80-kg person is, in the bottom position, a
  60-kg bench press — not "light bodyweight training." Second: the difference between standard and
  feet-highly-elevated is smaller than it feels. Decline push-ups feel heavier mainly because the load
  shifts toward the shoulder and upper chest, not because so much extra weight is added. Anyone who
  really wants to increase the load eventually needs unilateral variants or added weight — more in the
  article <a href="/en/blog/building-muscle-with-pushups">Building Muscle With Push-Ups</a>.
</p>

<h2>Hand position: what the EMG data show</h2>
<p>
  The common rule of thumb is: "wide for chest, narrow for triceps." The data tell a more nuanced
  story. In the most widely cited study on the topic,
  <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Cogley+2005+muscle+activation+hand+positions+push-up" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow ugc">Cogley et al. (2005)</a>
  had participants perform push-ups with narrow, shoulder-width, and wide hand positions and measured
  triceps and pectoralis major activation via surface EMG. Result: the <strong>narrow</strong> position
  produced the highest activation — in <em>both</em> muscles. The wide position was lowest for both.
</p>

<figure class="article-figure">
  <img src="/assets/blog/muskelaktivierung-handposition.svg" alt="Schematic ranking of EMG activation: narrow hand position activates triceps and chest the most, shoulder-width is medium, wide is lowest." width="900" height="520" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Relative ranking after Cogley et al. (2005). The bars show order, not absolute values.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p>
  Why is that? With a narrow hand position, the lever path is longer: the elbows travel farther, the
  upper arm moves through a greater range of horizontal adduction, and both muscles have to work over a
  longer distance. The wide position shortens that path — the rep gets easier, not more chest-focused. A
  second study
  (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Gouvali+Boudolos+2005+push-up+dynamic+analysis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow ugc">Gouvali &amp; Boudolos, 2005</a>)
  reached similar conclusions: hand position mainly changes the leverage at elbow and shoulder — the
  wide position offers no activation advantage.
</p>
<p>
  The practical takeaway: the shoulder-width push-up with elbows at roughly 45 degrees to the torso
  isn't the boring compromise — it's the biomechanical sweet spot of activation and joint health. Narrow
  variants like the <a href="/en/wiki/liegestuetz-typen/diamant">diamond push-up</a> are the choice when
  you want to maximally challenge both triceps <em>and</em> chest — and the wide position is more of an
  easier option for beginners than a chest-specialization tool.
</p>

<h2>Movement phase and tempo</h2>
<p>
  Every rep has two halves with very different characteristics:
</p>
<ol>
  <li>
    <strong>Eccentric (downward).</strong> The muscles lengthen under load. This phase is stronger in
    terms of force output — you could control significantly more weight lowering than pressing up — and
    it's the main driver of muscle soreness and growth stimulus. Beginners who can't yet do a single
    push-up should start with slow negatives: 3–5 seconds lowering, then back up with knees down.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Concentric (upward).</strong> The muscles shorten. The weakest point sits just above the
    floor, where the chest is maximally stretched — the "sticking point." This is where a rep succeeds
    or fails.
  </li>
</ol>
<p>
  Range of motion matters too. Full depth (chest a few centimeters above the floor) stretches the chest
  under load more, and that stretched position is currently considered especially effective for growth
  in hypertrophy research. Half reps rack up more numbers in the log but build less muscle. How to
  coordinate tempo with breathing is covered in the article
  <a href="/en/blog/pushup-breathing">How to Breathe Properly During Push-Ups</a>.
</p>

<h2>What push-ups don't train</h2>
<p>
  As complete as the exercise is for the front of the body, the back of your upper body stays largely
  untouched. The lats, rhomboids, middle and rear deltoids, and biceps get practically no work from
  push-ups. Anyone who only presses for months builds a muscular imbalance: shoulders pulled forward, a
  rounded upper back, and elevated risk for exactly the shoulder problems a strong serratus is supposed
  to prevent. Most trainers' rule of thumb is <strong>one pull per push</strong>: for every set of
  push-ups, one set of rows, pull-ups, or at least band pulls. The legs need their own exercises too —
  squats and lunges are in the <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen">exercise wiki</a>.
</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>
  A push-up is a pressing exercise for the chest, triceps, and front shoulder — but it's also a moving
  plank for the core, glutes, and shoulder-blade stabilizers. You move between half and three-quarters
  of your bodyweight in the process, and hand position shifts the workload less than the rules of thumb
  claim: narrow activates more, wide activates less, shoulder-width is the best compromise. Anyone who
  knows the anatomy can target the exercise deliberately — and knows where it hits its limits.
</p>
