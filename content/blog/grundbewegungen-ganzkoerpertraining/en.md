<h2>Why "muscle groups" is the wrong question</h2>
<p>
  Classic training planning thinks in muscles: chest on Monday, back on Tuesday, legs on Wednesday.
  That comes from bodybuilding, where it's about looks, and it works there too. For everyone else —
  people who want to be healthy, strong, and mobile — a different model has taken hold in training
  science since the 2000s: you don't plan by muscle, you plan by <strong>movement pattern</strong>. The
  body doesn't know a "chest exercise." It knows pushing, pulling, bending, extending, holding, and
  locomotion — and each of these patterns works many muscles at once, exactly the way they work
  together in everyday life.
</p>
<p>
  This approach has a huge practical advantage: it's complete. Anyone who trains every pattern every
  week has no gap in their program — no neglected backside, no forgotten legs, no core that's only
  worked "along the way." And it can be done entirely without equipment, which makes it appealing to
  anyone training at home, on the road, or in the park.
</p>

<h3>1. Push</h3>
<p>
  Everything where you move a load away from your body: horizontal (push-ups, bench press) or vertical
  (shoulder press, pike push-ups, handstand). Trains the chest, front shoulder, triceps — and, in the
  plank position, the entire core. Pushup Tracker takes its name from this pattern; which muscles
  exactly are working is covered in the article
  <a href="/en/blog/pushup-muscles-anatomy">Which Muscles Do Push-Ups Train?</a>
</p>

<h3>2. Pull</h3>
<p>
  The counterpart: load toward the body. Vertically, that's
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/pullups">pull-ups</a> and
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/dead-hang">dead hangs</a>; horizontally,
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/rows">rows</a> and <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/face-pull">face pulls</a>.
  Pulling trains the lats, rhomboids, rear shoulder, biceps, and grip strength — the muscles that
  push-only programs neglect and that are responsible for upright posture and healthy shoulders. The
  single most important rule of the whole model: <strong>one pull set for every push set.</strong>
</p>

<h3>3. Squat</h3>
<p>
  Knee- and hip-dominant bending with an upright torso: <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/squats">squats</a>,
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/lunges">lunges</a>, <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/step-ups">step-ups</a>,
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/wall-sit">wall sits</a>, <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/jump-squats">jump
  squats</a>. They build the thighs and glutes and are the movement that decides independence in old
  age: getting up from a chair, climbing stairs, picking something up off the floor. More on technique
  in the article <a href="/en/blog/squat-technique-guide">How to Squat With Proper Form</a>.
</p>

<h3>4. Hinge</h3>
<p>
  The most commonly forgotten movement: bending and extending from the hip with nearly straight knees.
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/glute-bridge">Glute bridges</a>,
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/single-leg-rdl">single-leg deadlifts</a>, and
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/good-morning">good mornings</a> train the entire backside — glutes,
  hamstrings, spinal erectors. Anyone who only squats develops a strong front side and a weak backside;
  the hinge pattern closes that gap and protects the lower back.
</p>

<h3>5. Core</h3>
<p>
  Not sit-ups, but stability: <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/plank">plank</a>,
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/dead-bug">dead bug</a>, <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/hollow-hold">hollow
  hold</a>, <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/leg-raises">leg raises</a>. The core's job in training is above
  all to secure the spine against movement while the arms and legs do the work. Why that's the case is
  explained in the article <a href="/en/blog/core-training-plank-dead-bug">Core Training Done Right</a>.
</p>

<h3>6. Endurance</h3>
<p>
  The cardiovascular system isn't a muscle, but it follows the same rules: it adapts to what you demand
  of it. <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/walking">Walking</a>,
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/running">running</a>, <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/cycling">cycling</a>,
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/jump-rope">jump rope</a>, <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/burpees">burpees</a>,
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/swimming">swimming</a>. Which intensity is for what is covered in the
  article <a href="/en/blog/hiit-vs-zone-2-cardio">HIIT or Zone 2?</a>
</p>

<h2>Why completeness matters more than perfection</h2>
<p>
  The large population health studies make one point clearly: it's not the best exercise that counts,
  but the combination. The
  <a href="https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240015128" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow ugc">WHO movement guidelines (2020)</a>
  recommend that adults get 150–300 minutes of moderate endurance activity per week <em>plus</em>
  strengthening exercises for all major muscle groups on at least two days. Anyone who does only one of
  the two gives up a large part of the effect: a
  <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Momma+2022+muscle-strengthening+activities+mortality+meta-analysis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow ugc">meta-analysis by Momma et al. (2022)</a>
  found that 30–60 minutes of strength training per week was associated with a 10–17% lower mortality
  risk — and that the combination of strength and endurance training lowered the risk more than either
  alone.
</p>
<p>
  The six-pattern model is the simplest way to guarantee that combination. It forces you to take your
  backside, legs, and heart just as seriously as the exercise you enjoy the most.
</p>

<h2>The balanced week</h2>
<p>
  Three sessions per week are enough to cover each pattern twice. The example below needs a pull-up bar
  or a sturdy table for rows — nothing else.
</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Day</th><th>Push</th><th>Pull</th><th>Squat / Hinge</th><th>Core</th><th>Endurance</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Monday</td><td>Push-ups 3 × 8–15</td><td>Rows 3 × 8–12</td><td>Squats 3 × 12–20</td><td>Plank 3 × 30–60 s</td><td>–</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Wednesday</td><td>–</td><td>–</td><td>–</td><td>–</td><td>30–45 min Zone 2 (running, cycling, brisk walking)</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Thursday</td><td>Pike push-ups 3 × 6–10</td><td>Pull-ups or negatives 3 × 3–8</td><td>Lunges 3 × 10 per side</td><td>Dead bug 3 × 8 per side</td><td>–</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Saturday</td><td>Dips or diamond push-ups 3 × 8–12</td><td>Rows 3 × 10–15</td><td>Glute bridge 3 × 15, single-leg RDL 3 × 8 per side</td><td>Hollow hold 3 × 20–40 s</td><td>Finisher: 10 min intervals (burpees, jump rope)</td></tr>
  </tbody>

<p>
  Each strength session takes 35–45 minutes. The rep ranges are chosen so each set ends with one to
  three reps in reserve — hard enough to drive adaptation, not so hard that form suffers. How to
  progressively increase within this framework is explained in the article
  <a href="/en/blog/building-muscle-with-pushups">Building Muscle With Push-Ups</a>; the principle
  applies to every pattern.
</p>

<h2>The most common imbalances — and how to spot them</h2>
<ul>
  <li>
    <strong>Lots of pushing, little pulling.</strong> The classic problem of push-up enthusiasts.
    Symptoms: rounded-forward shoulders, neck tension, eventually shoulder pain during pushing. Test:
    count your push and pull sets from last week. More than 3:2 in favor of pushing is a warning sign.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Only squatting, no hinging.</strong> Symptoms: strong front thighs, but back pain when
    lifting and glutes that barely engage when standing up. Test: can you hold a 30-second glute bridge
    without your hamstrings cramping?
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Strength without endurance.</strong> Symptoms: strong in a set, out of breath after two
    flights of stairs. Test: can you walk briskly or jog easily for 30 minutes while speaking in full
    sentences?
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Endurance without strength.</strong> The runner's pattern. Symptoms: a good heart, but
    fewer than 10 clean push-ups and knee trouble running downhill. Strength for legs and core is the
    best injury prevention for endurance athletes.
  </li>
</ul>

<h2>How to put the model into practice</h2>
<ol>
  <li><strong>Log every session — exercise, sets, and reps.</strong> The analysis tab shows you how
    your volume is distributed by category. A week without a pull session stands out immediately.</li>
  <li><strong>Plan by pattern, not by exercise.</strong> "Monday: push + pull + squat + core" is a plan
    you can execute even in a hotel room — you choose the specific exercise based on what's available.</li>
  <li><strong>Favor what you're neglecting.</strong> When two exercises are on the table, pick the one
    from the pattern that's weakest in your stats.</li>
  <li><strong>Keep the barrier to entry low.</strong> Two sets per pattern on three days is a complete
    program. More is a bonus, not a requirement.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>
  Six movement patterns, three sessions per week, no equipment except a bar or a table: this isn't a
  compromise program — it's the core of what movement science recommends for health and performance.
  The exercise you enjoy most is welcome to take center stage, as long as the other five patterns get
  their place every week.
</p>
