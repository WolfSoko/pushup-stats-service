<h2>The misunderstanding about ab training</h2>
<p>
  Almost everyone connects "core training" with a single picture: sit-ups, crunches, the burn in your
  abs, a six-pack as the goal. That picture is about fifty years old and has been outdated in training
  science for a good twenty years. The core — the entire musculature between the ribcage and the pelvis,
  front, side, and back — isn't a muscle you grow, it's a <strong>stabilization system</strong>. Its
  main job, in daily life and in sport, isn't to bend the torso, but to prevent the spine from moving
  while the arms and legs generate force.
</p>
<p>
  Anyone who understands that trains differently: less bending, more holding; fewer reps, more tension;
  and exercises that brace the core against outside forces rather than fatiguing it. This article
  explains the science behind that, places the plank, dead bug, hollow hold, and sit-up in context, and
  shows a progression that starts from zero.
</p>

<h2>What spine research says about sit-ups</h2>
<p>
  The Canadian biomechanist Stuart McGill has spent three decades measuring the forces various core
  exercises place on the lumbar spine. His central finding: the classic sit-up produces compressive
  forces on the discs well above 3,000 newtons — a value considered the threshold for repeated loading
  in occupational medicine. Repeated flexion under load is also exactly the mechanism that damages discs
  in lab studies. McGill's conclusion, detailed extensively in his textbook <em>Low Back Disorders</em>
  and in the
  <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=McGill+core+training+evidence+translating+to+better+performance+and+injury+prevention" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow ugc">review "Core Training: Evidence Translating to Better Performance and Injury Prevention" (2010)</a>:
  for most people, the sit-up is a bad trade — high disc risk for little benefit toward the function the
  core actually needs.
</p>
<p>
  That doesn't mean flexion is forbidden. A healthy back is allowed to bend, and exercises like crunches
  or leg raises have their place. It means flexion shouldn't be the <em>center</em> of core training —
  at most a quarter of it.
</p>

<h2>The four jobs of the core</h2>

<figure class="article-figure">
  <img src="/assets/blog/rumpf-funktionen.svg" alt="Four tiles: Anti-extension (plank, dead bug, push-up) prevents the lower back from arching; Anti-rotation (Pallof press, one-arm plank, archer) keeps the torso still under one-sided load; Anti-lateral-flexion (side plank, suitcase carry, single-leg RDL) stabilizes against sideways load; Flexion and extension (hollow hold, leg raises, crunches) creates movement. Rule of thumb: three-quarters anti-movement, one-quarter movement." width="900" height="500" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Three of the four jobs consist of preventing movement.</figcaption>
</figure>

<h3>1. Anti-extension</h3>
<p>
  The most important job. Whenever the arms or legs work away from the body — in a push-up, carrying
  something overhead, running — gravity pulls the lumbar spine into hyperextension. The abs and glutes
  push back against it. <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/plank">Plank</a> and
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/dead-bug">dead bug</a> are pure anti-extension exercises — and so is every
  push-up, as described in the article
  <a href="/en/blog/pushup-muscles-anatomy">Which Muscles Do Push-Ups Train?</a>
</p>

<h3>2. Anti-rotation</h3>
<p>
  When only one side is working — pressing with one arm, standing on one leg, carrying a bag in one
  hand — the torso wants to twist. The obliques prevent that. Exercises: Pallof press with a band, plank
  with one arm lifted, archer push-ups, one-arm rows.
</p>

<h3>3. Anti-lateral-flexion</h3>
<p>
  Load on one side — a child on your arm, a suitcase — tips the spine sideways. Side planks, suitcase
  carries (farmer's carry with one hand), and
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/single-leg-rdl">single-leg deadlifts</a> train the lateral core wall and
  the quadratus lumborum, which is involved in almost every case of back pain.
</p>

<h3>4. Flexion and extension</h3>
<p>
  The only job in which the core <em>generates</em> movement — and the only one covered by sit-ups and
  crunches. Used sparingly: <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/hollow-hold">hollow hold</a> (a hold in slight
  flexion, the gymnast's base position),
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/leg-raises">leg raises</a>, and
  <a href="/en/wiki/uebungen/crunches">crunches</a> with a small range of motion instead of a full
  sit-up.
</p>

<h2>The "Big Three" — the minimal program for a healthy back</h2>
<p>
  From his measurements, McGill distilled three exercises that cover all four jobs with minimal spinal
  load. They're now standard in back rehabilitation and a complete core program for anyone with ten
  minutes a day:
</p>
<ol>
  <li>
    <strong>Curl-up.</strong> Lying on your back, one knee bent, hands under your lower back. Lift your
    head and shoulders only a few centimeters — your lower back keeps its natural curve. Hold for 10
    seconds. This is the crunch that spares your discs.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Side plank.</strong> On your forearm, body in a straight line — on your knees at first, then
    on your feet. Hold for 10 seconds, switch sides.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Bird dog.</strong> On all fours, extend the opposite arm and leg horizontally, core
    absolutely still. Hold for 10 seconds, switch.
  </li>
</ol>
<p>
  The scheme: instead of long holds, several short 10-second reps — say 5, 3, 1 reps with short rests
  ("descending pyramid"). Core endurance comes from many short contractions, not one long one where form
  falls apart.
</p>

<h2>Plank: the most-performed and most-often-wrong exercise</h2>
<p>
  The plank is so simple it barely seems possible to get wrong — and yet most planks are ineffective.
  The difference is in the tension:
</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Engage your glutes and tuck your pelvis slightly.</strong> That removes the lower-back
    arch and shifts the work from the back to the abs. The plank instantly gets harder.</li>
  <li><strong>Actively press your elbows into the floor</strong> and pull your shoulder blades apart —
    that engages the serratus and protects the shoulder.</li>
  <li><strong>Tense your whole body:</strong> thighs, glutes, abs, fists. A good plank is uncomfortable
    after 20 seconds, not a three-minute wait.</li>
</ul>
<p>
  It follows from this logic: multi-hour plank world records are a matter of endurance and positioning,
  not core strength. In practice: if you can hold 60 seconds with maximal tension, don't hold longer —
  get harder. Lift an arm, lift a leg, elevate your feet, or switch to a dynamic variant like
  plank-to-push-up.
</p>

<p>
  Each stage typically takes two to four weeks with three short sessions per week. Core training tacks
  on well to any other session — ten minutes at the end is enough. The hollow-body position from the
  final stage is the foundation for pull-ups, handstands, and all advanced bodyweight exercises; anyone
  who masters it notices the difference immediately, as described in the article
  <a href="/en/blog/learning-pull-ups">Learning Pull-Ups</a>.
</p>

<h2>Core and back pain</h2>
<p>
  Nonspecific back pain is the most common reason for doctor visits in the working population. The
  evidence here is clear and sobering at once: movement helps, almost any movement —
  <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Owen+2020+which+specific+modes+of+exercise+training+are+most+effective+for+treating+low+back+pain+network+meta-analysis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow ugc">a network meta-analysis by Owen et al. (2020)</a>
  found similarly good effects for stabilization training, Pilates, and strength training, all clearly
  better than doing nothing. There's no specific miracle cure. What matters is consistency, avoiding
  repeated flexion under load during an acute phase, and building core endurance. The Big Three are the
  safest starting point for that. For radiating pain, numbness, or loss of strength in the legs, the
  back belongs in medical hands first.
</p>

<h2>What about the six-pack?</h2>
<p>
  Visible abs depend ninety percent on body fat percentage, not on training. A core that can hold a
  plank, side plank, and hollow hold has plenty of muscle for any six-pack — whether you can see it is
  decided in the kitchen. The advantage of the training described here is that it builds abdominal
  muscle without loading the spine; sit-ups aren't necessary for looks either.
</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>
  The core is a stabilization system: three of its four jobs consist of preventing movement. Good core
  training is therefore three-quarters holding against resistance — plank, side plank, dead bug, bird
  dog, hollow hold — and only one-quarter bending. Ten minutes, three times a week, through five stages
  from zero to hollow hold: that's all a healthy, resilient back needs.
</p>
