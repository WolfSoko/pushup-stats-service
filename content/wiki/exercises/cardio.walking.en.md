instructions:
  - Walk tall, steps natural, arms swinging.
  - Brisk pace — you can still talk but not sing.
  - Breathe through the nose if you can; switch to mouth+nose on inclines.
  - 30-60 minutes per session for endurance, or several short walks across the day.
tips:
  - 6-10 km/h on flat ground; hills bump the stimulus significantly.
  - Footwear matters more than pace — bad shoes wreck your gait after 30 minutes.

<h2>The most underrated cardio stimulus</h2>
<p>
  Walking seems too mundane to count — and for most people, it's the most effective way to start. At a
  brisk pace it already sits in zone 2 for untrained people, exactly the range where mitochondria and fat
  metabolism benefit the most. And it has an advantage no more intense form offers: you can do it every
  day without recovery becoming the bottleneck.
</p>
<h2>What "brisk" means</h2>
<p>
  The difference between strolling and training is pace. As a rule of thumb: breathing is clearly
  audible, full sentences are still possible, singing is not. If you're not slightly warm after ten
  minutes, you're going too slow.
</p>
<p>
  Hills are the simplest way to raise the intensity without having to go faster — often more comfortable
  than running for people with knee or hip issues.
</p>
<h2>What it's especially good for</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Getting started after a long break.</strong> Minimal joint load, real adaptation nonetheless.</li>
  <li><strong>Active recovery.</strong> The day after a hard session, an easy walk supports recovery instead of slowing it down.</li>
  <li><strong>Fitting into daily life.</strong> The commute, a phone call while walking — training that costs no extra time actually gets done.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Programming</h2>
<p>
  30 to 60 minutes on most days. To do more, increase duration first, then incline, then pace — in that
  order.
</p>
