name: Nordic Walking
summary: Walking with poles — brings the upper body in and raises energy expenditure noticeably.
instructions:
  - Pole length roughly your height × 0.66; when in doubt, take the shorter option.
  - Plant the pole diagonally next to the foot and push off behind you — never plant it ahead of your body.
  - Open the hand at the end of the push; the strap holds the pole and spares your forearm and shoulder.
  - Lean the torso slightly forward, shoulders down, core engaged.
tips:
  - The most common mistake is simply carrying the poles — without a real push-off it's just walking with added weight.
  - Rubber tips on asphalt, the metal spike off-road.
---

<h2>Walking that brings the upper body along</h2>
<p>
  Nordic walking has a reputation as a retirees' pastime — and is in fact one of the few ways to turn
  a very low-impact movement into a whole-body stimulus. Done properly, the pole push involves the
  lats, triceps and core on every stride. Energy expenditure sits noticeably above plain walking at
  the same pace, without adding any load to knees or hips.
</p>
<h2>The push-off is what counts</h2>
<p>
  The difference between training and strolling with poles comes down to one movement: the pole is
  pushed off diagonally behind the body, not planted in front of it. Carry the poles along and you're
  taking weight for a walk.
</p>
<p>
  A good test: after ten minutes you should feel the back of your shoulder. If you don't, the push is
  missing.
</p>
<h2>What it's especially good for</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Low-impact endurance.</strong> The poles take load off knees and hips on every stride.</li>
  <li><strong>Returning after injury.</strong> More stable than free walking, more stimulus than strolling.</li>
  <li><strong>Shoulder and neck mobility.</strong> The rhythmic arm work loosens what desk work locks up.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Dosage</h2>
<p>
  30 to 60 minutes, two or three times a week. Technique before pace: give the first few sessions to
  the push-off and the rest follows.
</p>
