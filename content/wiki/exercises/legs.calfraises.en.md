---
name: Calf Raises
summary: Isolated calf training — jump power and stability for ankles and knees.
instructions:
  - Stand tall, feet hip-width, weight on the balls of the feet.
  - Press slowly up onto the toes, pause briefly at the top.
  - Lower under control — do not let the heels crash into the floor.
  - Standing on a step edge unlocks the full range of motion.
tips:
  - Stand on a step (heels hanging) for much more stretch and stimulus.
  - High reps work better for calves — aim for 15-25 per set.
---

<h2>The underrated exercise for ankles and jump power</h2>
<p>
  The calves rarely get trained directly, yet they're involved in almost every form of locomotion. They
  supply the final push-off, cushion the landing on every step, and stabilize the ankle. Runners benefit
  twice over: more propulsion and noticeably less susceptibility to Achilles tendon and shin problems.
</p>
<h2>Execution</h2>
<p>
  Standing, feet hip-width, weight on the balls of the feet. Press slowly up onto the toes as high as
  possible, hold for a second at the top, then lower under control — deeper than the starting position if
  you're standing on a step. That stretched range at the bottom is where most of the effect comes from.
</p>
<p>
  With the knee straight, the two-headed gastrocnemius does most of the work; with the knee bent, the
  soleus underneath takes over. Both variants earn their place.
</p>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Bouncing.</strong> The calf is fast-twitch and loves to spring. Without tempo control, hardly any training stimulus happens.</li>
  <li><strong>Range too small.</strong> The lower, stretched range is the most valuable part — a step or a board is worth using.</li>
  <li><strong>Too few reps.</strong> The calf tolerates and needs more volume than most muscles.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Programming</h2>
<p>
  Three to four sets of 15 to 25 reps, slow. Performed single-leg, the exercise gets noticeably harder
  and also reveals side-to-side differences.
</p>
