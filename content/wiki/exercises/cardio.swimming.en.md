---
name: Swimming
summary: Full-body cardio in the water — joint-friendly and breath-demanding.
instructions:
  - Body horizontal — bring the hips and legs close to the surface.
  - Breathe sideways every 2-3 strokes (freestyle), or forward (breaststroke).
  - Kick from the hips, not the knees — small, fast strokes.
  - Long, even pulls beat thrashing — efficiency over power.
tips:
  - Start with short distances (4 × 50 m) and rest, rather than chasing one 200 m attempt.
  - Goggles cut friction and irritation dramatically — non-negotiable kit.
---

<h2>Full body and weightless</h2>
<p>
  Swimming is the only widespread endurance sport with zero impact loading — buoyancy takes essentially
  all the body's weight off. For people with joint issues, significant excess weight, or in
  rehabilitation, it's often the only form that's pain-free at all.
</p>
<p>
  At the same time, it's more demanding on breathing than any land-based sport: water pressure on the
  chest makes inhaling harder, and the breathing rhythm is locked to the stroke rather than freely
  chosen.
</p>
<h2>Technique beats conditioning</h2>
<p>
  Unlike running, in swimming it's almost always technique that limits you, not endurance. If you're out
  of breath after fifty meters, it's usually not a conditioning problem but a body position sitting too
  low with too much drag. A few technique lessons help more here than extra laps.
</p>
<h2>What to keep in mind</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Heart rate runs lower.</strong> Lying horizontal in cool water, the heart beats roughly ten beats slower than on land at the same effort. Running zones don't transfer directly.</li>
  <li><strong>No bone stimulus.</strong> As with cycling, there's no impact loading for bone density — strength training belongs alongside it.</li>
  <li><strong>Neck in breaststroke.</strong> A constantly hyperextended head is a common source of complaints. Dip the head into the water regularly.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Programming</h2>
<p>
  Two to three sessions of 30 to 45 minutes. Intervals over 50 or 100 meters with clear rest periods are
  usually more effective than continuous laps.
</p>
