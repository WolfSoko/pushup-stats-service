---
name: Handstand Hold
summary: Static handstand against a wall or free — shoulder strength, stability and full-body tension.
instructions:
  - Kneel in front of an open wall, hands shoulder-width, fingertips about a hand's width from the wall.
  - Kick one leg up, the other follows — heels rest lightly against the wall.
  - Hold the body as a straight line — hips and chest stacked over the hands, glutes braced.
  - Lower back down before the shoulders fatigue.
tips:
  - 'Beginners: build pike push-ups and wall walks first, then start practising the hold.'
  - Press the shoulders actively "into the ears" — no sunken position.
---

<h2>From pressing to carrying</h2>
<p>
  The handstand hold isn't a pressing exercise, it's a holding exercise: the shoulder carries the full
  body weight in an overhead position while the trunk and wrists stabilize the line. It builds shoulder
  stability that no other bodyweight exercise creates the same way, and it's the prerequisite for the
  handstand push-up.
</p>
<p>
  The path there runs through the pike push-up: the higher the feet are placed there, the more vertical
  the load becomes — the handstand is the endpoint of that line.
</p>
<h2>Execution against a wall</h2>
<p>
  Two variants. Walking up facing the wall gives the straighter, more honest line but is harder to get
  into. Facing away from the wall is the easier entry but tempts you into a lower-back arch.
</p>
<p>
  In both cases: hands shoulder-width, fingers spread and actively gripping the floor, arms straight,
  shoulders pressed up toward the ears. Glutes and abs braced, ribs down — the body is a line, not an
  arch.
</p>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Lower-back arch.</strong> The most common mistake and the reason many can't hold the position — they're fighting their own posture.</li>
  <li><strong>Sunken shoulders.</strong> Actively push up, otherwise the weight hangs in the joints.</li>
  <li><strong>Cold wrists.</strong> Circle and stretch them before the first rep; the load is unusually high.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Programming</h2>
<p>
  Four to six sets of 15 to 45 seconds with generous rest. Train it fresh, not at the end of a session.
</p>
