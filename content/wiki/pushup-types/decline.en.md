---
name: Decline push-up (feet elevated)
summary: Feet on an elevation, hands on the floor — extra load on the upper chest and shoulders.
instructions:
  - Feet on a stable elevation (bench, chair, step).
  - Hands shoulder-width on the floor, keep a straight line from head to heels.
  - Lower the chest under control, keep the abs braced.
  - Press up powerfully — the higher the feet, the more shoulder involvement.
tips:
  - Do not let the lower back arch — actively brace abs and glutes, especially with high feet.
---

<h2>What elevating the feet actually changes</h2>
<p>
  Decline push-ups are seen as the obvious progression after the standard push-up — and they are one, but
  for a different reason than usually assumed. The share of body weight on the hands rises from 65-75
  percent to about 70 percent with a moderate elevation and around 75 percent with a high one. That's
  noticeable, but it doesn't explain why the variation feels so much harder.
</p>
<p>
  The real difference lies in how the load is distributed: the higher the feet, the more the work shifts
  from the middle and lower chest toward the shoulder and upper chest. As foot height increases, the
  push-up starts to resemble an overhead press — until, in the pike push-up, it becomes almost vertical.
</p>
<h2>Execution</h2>
<p>
  The feet rest on a stable elevation, hands shoulder-width under the shoulders. The higher the feet, the
  stronger the tendency for the torso to sag into an arch — glutes and abs stay active, ribs pull slightly
  down. The head stays in line with the spine, even though the gaze now points more steeply downward.
</p>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Too high, too soon.</strong> A step of 20 to 40 centimeters is plenty for the first stage. Going straight to hip height usually means losing core tension.</li>
  <li><strong>Arched lower back.</strong> The most common mistake in this variation. If the hips sag, the elevation is too high or the core is too fatigued.</li>
  <li><strong>Overloading the shoulder.</strong> Anyone who already has issues with overhead movements should raise the foot height cautiously — that's exactly where the load shifts.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Where it fits</h2>
<p>
  As a progression after the standard push-up, this variation works well, but it has a ceiling: you can't
  push more than about 75 percent of body weight onto the hands through foot height alone. Anyone who
  needs more load has to turn to unilateral variations or added weight. Three to four sets of six to
  fifteen reps is a good range.
</p>
