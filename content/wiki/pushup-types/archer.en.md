<h2>The bridge between two arms and one</h2>
<p>
  In the archer push-up, the hands sit noticeably wider than shoulder-width. You lower to one side so
  that one arm bends and takes on most of the work, while the other stays extended and only provides
  support. The name comes from the position — it resembles a drawn bow.
</p>
<p>
  The practical value lies in being able to dial the load through the support arm: the less it helps
  press, the closer the exercise gets to the one-arm push-up. And unlike negatives, you train the full
  movement — lowering and pressing.
</p>
<h2>Execution</h2>
<p>
  Hands about one and a half to two shoulder-widths apart. Lower to one side until the chest comes close
  to the working hand; the other arm stays mostly straight, with the palm allowed to rotate slightly or
  shift onto the fingertips. The body stays in one line — the hips don't rotate.
</p>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>The support arm presses along.</strong> The most common mistake — and it makes the exercise pointless. The straight arm stabilizes; it doesn't work.</li>
  <li><strong>Hips rotate open.</strong> As with all unilateral variations, this is the central issue. Shoulders and hips stay parallel to the floor.</li>
  <li><strong>Uneven volume.</strong> Do both sides the same number of times, even if one is clearly stronger.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Where it fits</h2>
<p>
  The archer push-up is the most versatile step in the progression, because you can fine-tune it both
  through hand width and through how much the support arm contributes. Three to four sets of five to ten
  reps per side is a solid range. Anyone working cleanly here has already built most of the strength
  needed for the one-arm variation.
</p>
