---
name: Incline push-up (hands elevated)
summary: Hands on a bench, table or wall — reduces the load on the arms.
instructions:
  - Place the hands on a stable elevation (bench, table, stair).
  - Feet on the floor, body in a straight line.
  - Lower the chest under control to the elevation, then press up.
  - The lower the elevation, the harder the exercise.
tips:
  - Step the elevation down once 3×10 reps feel clean.
---

<h2>The most important entry-level variation</h2>
<p>
  Incline push-ups are the most reliable path from a first attempt to a full push-up — precisely because
  they're infinitely adjustable. The higher the surface, the less body weight rests on the hands: about
  40 percent at a high elevation, around 55 percent at a low one, compared with 65-75 percent for a
  push-up on the floor. Between the wall and the floor lies an entire spectrum you can fine-tune through
  the height of the surface.
</p>
<p>
  Compared to the knee push-up, the incline variation has a decisive advantage: the body stays fully
  extended. You train core tension from the start instead of having to learn it later.
</p>
<h2>Finding the right height</h2>
<p>
  Choose a height that lets you do eight to twelve clean reps, with the last two feeling noticeably hard.
  A kitchen counter, a sturdy table, a windowsill, a stair step, or a weight bench — any stable edge works,
  as long as it won't slide out or tip. With stairs you get the advantage that the next progression step
  is literally one step lower.
</p>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Hips sag.</strong> Because the exercise is easier, core tension fades faster than it does on the floor. Keep glutes and abs active, body in one line.</li>
  <li><strong>Dropping a level too soon.</strong> Reducing the height before you can do three sets of ten clean reps just accumulates half-reps instead of progress.</li>
  <li><strong>Unstable surface.</strong> A chair that can tip is not a training station. The surface has to hold under full weight.</li>
</ul>
<h2>How you get to the full push-up</h2>
<p>
  Work at one height until three sets of ten clean reps succeed, then drop a level. Each jump feels
  noticeably harder — that's normal, and the rep count can temporarily drop. Two to three sessions per
  week are enough. Most beginners need anywhere from a few weeks to a few months to go from the wall to
  the floor, depending on starting level and consistency.
</p>
